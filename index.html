Prototype writeup

What have we done so far?

We collected and cleaned two public datasets: American Community Survey 1-year estimates for 414 U.S. metro areas from 2005 to 2025, and national FRED series for home prices, household income, rent, and overall inflation going back to 1984. Because metro-area codes and names change between Census years, we wrote a Python script that matches each metro by its principal city and state, so a city's history lines up across all 20 years. We laid out the full and-but-therefore structure of the page and built three working D3 visualizations. The year slider re-bases the national chart to the year your parents turned 22, the city picker drives the rent-burden chart and its summary numbers, and the scatterplot lets readers hover over any metro and click it to make it their city. We also added annotations and tooltips so each chart states its main finding in plain language.

What will be the most challenging part to design, and why?

The hardest part will be making the "then vs. now" comparison fair at the city level. Our city data starts in 2005, but most of our readers' parents turned 22 between the mid-1980s and early 2000s, so we need to bring in the 1990 and 2000 decennial Census tables and line up their geography and dollar values with the ACS. A second challenge is choosing a measure of affordability that is honest: rent as a share of income depends on whose income you use, and a young adult's income is very different from the median household's, so we may use microdata for 22–25-year-olds. Finally, the down-payment calculator has to rest on assumptions about savings rates, so we need to design it so readers can see and change those assumptions instead of trusting a single number.
